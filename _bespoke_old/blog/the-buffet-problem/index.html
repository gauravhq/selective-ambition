The Buffet Problem

By Gaurav Srivastava

Why more options quietly make us less happy.

Picture an all-you-can-eat buffet. A hundred dishes, all included. The first lap is exciting. By the third, something curdles. You are not enjoying any single thing; you are anxiously trying not to miss a better thing two trays down. You leave full and strangely dissatisfied.

Modern life is that buffet, and AI just added a thousand new dishes. The psychologist Barry Schwartz called this the paradox of choice: past a point, more options do not free us, they paralyse us. In a well-known study by Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper, shoppers offered six jams were far more likely to actually buy one than shoppers offered twenty-four. More choice, less action, less satisfaction.

The answer is not more discipline. It is taste: the willingness to put two things on your plate, eat them slowly, and let the rest go. Abundance is not the same as freedom. Sometimes it is just a prettier prison, and the way out is choosing less, on purpose.

This is one idea from Selective Ambition: The Joy of Missing Out on Purpose by Gaurav Srivastava — the case for caring deeply about fewer things, and letting the rest go on purpose.
