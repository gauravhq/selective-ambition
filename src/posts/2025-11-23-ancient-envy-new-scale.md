---
title: "Ancient Envy, New Scale"
date: 2025-11-23
pageDescription: "The urge to envy a neighbour is ancient, but the scale of comparison is entirely new."
---
The impulse to look sideways and measure yourself against the person next to you is not new. It is not even close to new.

The Romans had a word for it: invidia, envy of a neighbour's good fortune, common enough to need its own name. Dante placed it on the second terrace of Purgatory, a weight the envious carried as punishment for a lifetime of sideways looking. Medieval monks wrote entire treatises warning against the danger of measuring your own worth against another person's station, as if the habit were serious enough to deserve a chapter of its own, centuries before anyone had a phone to blame.

So the urge itself is ancient. Every generation before this one has had to wrestle with some version of the same pull, the same sideways glance, the same quiet arithmetic of who has more.

What changed is not the impulse. What changed is the scale. A monk in a small stone room measured himself against a handful of other monks he saw every day. Your phone hands you millions of comparisons before you have even finished breakfast, curated and endless, and none of them know a single thing about your actual life.
